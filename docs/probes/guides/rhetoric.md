# Guide: rhetoric

Version: v2. Read `common.md` first.

This guide has seven labels. A post can get several `yes`, or none.

## Shared condition S: the post is about a public matter

Every label of this guide needs condition S. S is met when the post is about politics, government, a public policy, society, the economy, health, science, security, war, the media, an institution, or the conduct of a company or a public figure.

S is **not** met by a post that is only about the author's private life, entertainment, sport, hobbies, a product for sale, or a greeting.

If S is not met, answer `no` to all seven labels.

Evidence for every label of this guide: required.

## `loaded_language`

**Question.** Does the post name or describe something with a charged word where a neutral word exists?

**YES when all are true:**

1. S is met.
2. The post names or describes a person, a group, an organisation, an act, an event, a policy or a situation with a word or phrase that carries a strong emotional or moral charge.
3. A neutral word for the same thing exists, and putting it in keeps the fact and removes the charge. Name the neutral word to yourself: if you cannot, answer `no`. Examples of pairs: "regime" for government, "thugs" for protesters, "invasion" for arrivals, "slaughter" for killing, "handout" for benefit, "witch hunt" for inquiry, "puppet" for ally, "freedom fighters" for rebels.

**NO when:**

- the word is a plain evaluation: good, bad, great, terrible, awful, best, worst, love, hate;
- the word is a bare insult about intelligence or looks: idiot, stupid, ugly, clown;
- the only candidate is a hashtag.

**Examples.**

- YES: "The regime rammed its tax grab through parliament last night" → `tax grab`
- YES: "Another invasion of illegals at the border this week" → `invasion of illegals`
- NO: "The government passed a terrible tax law last night" (a plain evaluation)
- NO: "The minister is an idiot" (a bare insult)

## `exaggeration`

**Question.** Does the post overstate or play down beyond what it supports?

**YES when all are true:**

1. S is met.
2. The post makes a statement about size, number, frequency, importance or consequence.
3. It does so in absolute or extreme terms that nothing in the post supports ("everyone", "nobody", "all", "nothing", "always", "never", "not at all", "completely", "totally", "the worst in history", "the end of"), or it plays down something the post itself presents as serious ("just a", "nothing more than", "no big deal").

Judge the wording literally: do not ask whether the author "really means" the absolute term. The one exception is rule 4 of `common.md`: an absolute said in obvious sarcasm is not claimed.

**NO when:**

- the extreme term comes with a figure or a fact that supports it in the post;
- the statement is a measured comparison ("higher than last year").

**Examples.**

- YES: "Nobody in this country trusts the courts anymore" → `Nobody in this country trusts the courts anymore`
- YES: "This bill is the end of free speech" → `the end of free speech`
- YES: "Thousands lost their savings but it's just a small glitch, relax" → `just a small glitch`
- NO: "Trust in the courts fell to 38%, the lowest since 1990" (supported by a figure)

## `fear_appeal`

**Question.** Does the post use a danger as the reason to support or reject something?

**YES when all are true:**

1. S is met.
2. The post warns of a harm **to come** for the readers, a group or society: a future ("will", "next", "are coming for"), a condition ("if … then"), or a question that puts the reader in the victim's place ("would you accept …?").
3. It uses that harm as the reason to support or reject a person, a group, a policy or an idea: "if X wins, …", "X will destroy …", "they are coming for your …".

**NO when:**

- the post is a factual warning or safety advice with nothing to support or reject (weather, a product recall, a scam alert);
- the post describes a harm that has already happened or is happening, with no warning about what comes ("people are dying because of this policy");
- the post reports a danger without tying it to a side;
- the author only says they are afraid.

**Examples.**

- YES: "If this law passes, your children won't be safe in their own schools" → `your children won't be safe in their own schools`
- YES: "They are coming for your savings next. Stop them before it's too late" → `They are coming for your savings next`
- NO: "Storm warning tonight: winds up to 120 km/h, stay indoors" (safety advice)
- NO: "Experts fear a recession next year" (a report)

## `doubt`

**Question.** Does the post question someone's credibility by insinuation, without giving a ground?

**YES when all are true:**

1. S is met.
2. The post questions the honesty, the competence or the motives of a person, an organisation or an institution.
3. It does so with one of these forms, and states no specific fact as a ground:
   - a rhetorical question about trust, motives or hidden things: "Can we really trust …?", "What is she hiding?", "Who is paying them?", "Just a coincidence?";
   - an insinuating opener: "Funny how …", "Strange that …", "Interesting that …", "Makes you wonder";
   - a distancing word on the target's claim or title: "so-called", "supposedly", "self-proclaimed", scare quotes on a title (the "expert").

If none of these forms is in the post, answer `no`.

**NO when:**

- the post states a specific act as a ground ("he lied about the budget in March");
- the post only insults;
- the question is a real request for information ("Is this source reliable?").

**Examples.**

- YES: "Who is really funding this so-called charity? Makes you wonder" → `Who is really funding this so-called charity?`
- YES: "Funny how the minister never shows the full report" → `Funny how the minister never shows the full report`
- NO: "The charity's accounts show two million missing since 2023" (a stated fact)
- NO: "Does anyone know who funds this charity?" (a real question)

## `identity_appeal`

**Question.** Does the post use belonging to a nation or a group as the reason to support or do something?

**YES when all are true:**

1. S is met.
2. The post invokes belonging to a nation, a people, a religion, a region or another large identity group: "as Americans", "real patriots", "our people", "true Christians", "for the fatherland".
3. It uses that belonging as the reason to support, reject or do something, or as the proof of who is right.

**NO when:**

- the group is only mentioned ("I'm German and I live in Berlin");
- the post expresses pride, or lists the achievements of a nation or a group, and argues for nothing ("Proud to be Canadian today", "We built the longest bridge in the world").

**Examples.**

- YES: "Any real patriot knows this treaty must be rejected" → `Any real patriot knows`
- YES: "As Christians we cannot accept this law" → `As Christians we cannot accept this law`
- NO: "Proud of our team, proud of this country" (pride, nothing promoted)
- NO: "French voters go to the polls on Sunday" (a mention)

## `us_vs_them`

**Question.** Does the post set a good "us" against a bad "them"?

**YES when all are true:**

1. S is met.
2. The post names or points to two camps: a collective that includes the author ("we", "us", "our", "the people", "ordinary folks", a group the author says they belong to) and another ("they", "them", "the elites", "those people", a named group). "I" alone is not a camp.
3. It gives virtue or victimhood to the first and blame or malice to the second, as groups.

**NO when:**

- the other side is one individual;
- the two groups only disagree, with no moral contrast;
- only one camp appears.

**Examples.**

- YES: "We work, we pay, and they sit in their palaces laughing at us" → `they sit in their palaces laughing at us`
- YES: "The elites hate ordinary people like us and always have" → `The elites hate ordinary people like us`
- NO: "We disagree with the unions on the retirement age" (no moral contrast)
- NO: "The president doesn't care about us" (one individual)

## `slogan`

**Question.** Does the post contain a slogan?

**YES when all are true:**

1. S is met.
2. The post contains a short, self-contained formula of eight words at most, which a crowd could chant or print on a sign unchanged, or a hashtag made of several words that reads as such a formula.
3. The formula states a demand or a stance: "Build the wall", "No justice, no peace", "My body, my choice", "Stop the steal", `#NotMyPresident`.

**NO when:**

- the hashtag only names a topic, a place or an event (`#election`, `#Brexit`, `#COVID19`);
- the sentence is ordinary prose, not a formula;
- the formula is a brand tagline.

**Examples.**

- YES: "Enough is enough. No justice, no peace!" → `No justice, no peace!`
- YES: "Counting the days #VoteThemOut" → `#VoteThemOut`
- NO: "I think the wall should be built along the whole border" (ordinary prose)
- NO: "Watching the debate tonight #election" (a topic hashtag)
